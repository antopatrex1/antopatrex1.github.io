Thesis

Why most of physical AI is going the wrong way

The dominant bet is that a big enough model trained on enough egocentric video produces a general-purpose robot. Hundreds of millions are going into it. I think it's wrong, and the evidence is already visible to anyone willing to look at a deployment instead of a benchmark.

Lab accuracy does not survive contact with a building. A policy that performs beautifully on a bench fails in a corridor, because the corridor has a wet floor sign, a resident with a stroller, an elevator that closes early, and a parcel shaped like nothing in the training set. Divergence from the expected isn't an edge case out there. It's the entire job.

Nobody solved hard autonomy in simulation. Waymo spent fourteen years driving. Zoox built its competence around forensics on what went wrong in the field. Both got there by deploying imperfect systems under supervision and closing the loop on every failure. That path is open in robotics right now and almost nobody is walking it.

So invert the sequence. Put robots into real environments now, keep a human in the loop so nothing unsafe happens while they're still bad, and let deployment data compound. Humanoids belong first in semi-private space, hotels and apartment buildings, because that's the closest thing to a home that still has an operator, a budget, and a reason to care.

The useful consequence: scaling laws haven't landed in physical AI yet, which means a seed-stage company and a billion-dollar lab are on genuinely level ground. What decides it isn't compute. It's who gets robots into buildings first and learns fastest from what breaks.
